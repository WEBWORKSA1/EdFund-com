What is the FAFSA?

The Free Application for Federal Student Aid (FAFSA) is the U.S. Department of Education's form for determining eligibility for federal student aid: Pell Grants, Federal Work-Study, and Direct Loans. Most states and many colleges also use it to award their own grants and scholarships. It's free to file at StudentAid.gov. Never pay a website to file it.

Who should file?

Almost everyone planning to attend college or career school in the U.S., whatever the family income. Many merit scholarships and state programs require a FAFSA on file, and federal student loans (which don't require a credit check) are only available through it.

💡 Myth: "My parents earn too much." There's no income cutoff for filing, and many families are surprised by what they qualify for.

What you need before you start

  • A StudentAid.gov account (FSA ID) for the student and each required contributor (e.g., parents of dependent students)
  • Social Security numbers (if you have them)
  • Federal tax information from two years before the award year (usually transferred directly from the IRS with your consent)
  • Records of assets: cash, savings, investments, and business or farm value where required
  • A list of colleges you're considering (you can add several)

Step by step

  1. Create accounts. Student and contributors each create a StudentAid.gov account. Do this a few days early, because identity verification can take time.
  2. Start the form. The student starts the FAFSA and invites contributors by email.
  3. Provide consent. Each contributor must consent to have federal tax information transferred from the IRS. Without consent, the FAFSA is incomplete and no aid can be calculated.
  4. Fill in your sections. The student and contributors each complete their own parts.
  5. List schools. Add every college you're considering; each will receive your results.
  6. Sign and submit. Then watch for your FAFSA Submission Summary and any requests from colleges.

Understanding your Student Aid Index (SAI)

Your FAFSA produces a Student Aid Index, an eligibility number colleges use to build your aid package. A lower SAI means more need-based aid. The SAI can be negative (down to −1500), which signals the highest need. Pell Grant eligibility is tied to the SAI, family size and income.

Deadlines that matter

  • When it opens: the FAFSA for the next school year typically becomes available around October 1.
  • Federal deadline: June 30 at the end of the award year. But don't wait for it.
  • State and college deadlines: often much earlier, and some state aid is first-come, first-served. Check your state agency and each college's financial aid page.

7 tips to maximize your aid

  1. File as early as you can after it opens.
  2. Add every school you're considering.
  3. Report assets accurately. Retirement accounts and your primary home are generally not reported as assets.
  4. Double-check household size. It changes your SAI.
  5. If your family's finances changed (job loss, medical costs), ask each college's aid office about a professional judgment review.
  6. Also file the CSS Profile if a private college requires it.
  7. Keep applying to scholarships. Outside awards reduce what you'll need to borrow.
