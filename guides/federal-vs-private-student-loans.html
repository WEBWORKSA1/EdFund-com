Federal vs. private student loans

The order you borrow in can save you thousands of dollars. Here's the rule: free money → federal loans → private loans.

Federal student loans

Federal loans come from the U.S. Department of Education through the FAFSA. For 2026–27 (first disbursed July 1, 2026 – June 30, 2027), fixed rates are:

  • 6.52%: Direct Subsidized & Unsubsidized (undergraduate)
  • 8.07%: Direct Unsubsidized (graduate/professional)
  • 9.07%: Direct PLUS (parents; grad PLUS for eligible existing borrowers)

Key advantages: no credit check for most student loans, income-driven repayment options, deferment and forbearance, and eligibility for forgiveness programs like Public Service Loan Forgiveness. Subsidized loans don't accrue interest while you're in school at least half-time.

⚠️ Federal borrowing rules for graduate students and parents changed starting July 1, 2026. Confirm your limits at StudentAid.gov.

Private student loans

Private loans come from banks, credit unions and online lenders. Your rate depends on your credit (or your cosigner's), and it can be fixed or variable. The best-qualified borrowers may beat federal rates, but protections vary widely by lender.

When private loans make sense

  • You've used up scholarships, grants and federal loans and still have a gap.
  • You (or a cosigner) have strong credit and can get a rate well below the PLUS rate.
  • You're an international student without access to federal aid.

How to shop for a private loan

  1. Prequalify with several lenders. Soft credit checks don't hurt your score.
  2. Compare the APR, not just the rate, and check fees.
  3. Prefer fixed rates if you want predictable payments.
  4. Check cosigner release, forbearance and death/disability discharge policies.
  5. Borrow only what you need. Use the affordability calculator.
